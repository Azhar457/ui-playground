ABOUT MOVEMENT

      

        


        
BRIDGET RILEY — RETINA BEFORE EYE

        

          Riley (b. 1931, London) translated her post-war
          experiments with grey-scale dots and stripes into hard-edged geometry: works like
          Movement in Squares (1961) and Fall (1963) send the grid itself
          into compression and release. Her paintings vibrate because the eye cannot resolve
          the spatial rhythm; the brain keeps re-scanning, and the afterimage does the rest.
        


        
VICTOR VASARELY — THE GEOMETRIC PARENT

        

          Vasarely (1906–1997), the self-declared "father of
          Op Art", industrialized illusion. In Zebra (1937) and the later
          Planetary Folklore series he locked black/white or complementary colour
          pairs into systems where shape alone generates depth. He treated the canvas as a
          programmed surface decades before the computer made that metaphor literal.
        


        
WHY COMPLEMENTARY PAIRS VIBRATE

        

          Opponent-colour cells in the retina fire against each other at exactly
          complementary hues — red against cyan, blue against yellow, green against magenta.
          Set those fields edge to edge and the border tremor you see is your own neural wiring
          reacting, not the screen. The four theme pairs on this page each tune a different
          opponent channel. The stare-dot test above is the same mechanism shown in time:
          fatigue one channel, release it, and the phantom colour appears.
