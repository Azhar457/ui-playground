Topological edge modes in biological photosynthetic complexes under ambient thermal decoherence

Elena Rostova1,2*, Marcus Thorne1, Kenji Takahashi3 & David Chen2
1Department of Physics, Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge, UK.
2Max Planck Institute for Quantum Optics, Garching, Germany.
3Institute for Solid State Physics, University of Tokyo, Kashiwa, Japan.

Abstract Non-trivial topological invariants have recently revolutionized solid-state physics, yet their presence and robustness in wet, noisy biological systems remain fiercely debated. Here we demonstrate experimentally that excitation energy transfer in the FMO pigment-protein complex exhibits protected chiral edge modes that suppress thermal decoherence at physiological temperatures (298 K). Our findings suggest biological evolution harnesses topological protection for near-unity energy transfer efficiency.
Keywords: topological photonics • quantum biology • excitation energy transfer • non-Hermitian systems • 2DES spectroscopy

Introduction

Photosynthetic light-harvesting complexes operate with quantum efficiencies approaching 98%, despite existing in warm, fluctuating molecular environments[1]. Traditional Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) models fail to account for this resilience under strong vibrational coupling[2].

In this study, we map the chromophore coupling matrix of the Fenna-Matthews-Olson (FMO) trimer[8] to a two-dimensional Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) Hamiltonian with non-Hermitian parity-time symmetry[9]. We demonstrate that the highest energy transfer rates coincide with non-zero Chern numbers (\(C = 1\)), extending the solid-state classification of topological insulators[3] into the biological regime.

Fig. 1 | Spectroscopic verification of topological protection. 2D electronic spectroscopy traces recorded across the 770–830 nm absorption band showing zero decay on the boundary chromophore nodes.

Results & Topology Mapping

By performing ultrafast two-dimensional electronic coherence spectroscopy (2DES) with 12-fs optical pulses, we resolved quantum beating signatures that persisted over 800 fs[4]. The edge-mode population remained invariant against synthetic conformational distortions, consistent with the robustness criterion for topologically protected transport[11].

The absorption manifold shown in Fig. 2 isolates the Qx band that serves as the coherent excitation source. When the probe window was swept across 77–320 K, the topological channel retained >96% of its room-temperature flux while the incoherent FRET channel collapsed by 41% (Fig. 3).

Fig. 2 | Absorption cross-section of the FMO trimer. The dominant Qx band at 808 nm overlaps the 12-fs 2DES probe window, enabling full chromophore resolution.

Fig. 3 | Transport efficiency across the physiological range. Topological transport remains above 96% to 320 K, whereas FRET models predict a linear collapse with temperature.

Discussion

Our results place the FMO complex on a two-dimensional topological phase boundary whose protection is guaranteed by symmetry rather than by fine-tuned molecular geometry[5]. Environment-assisted quantum transport models[6] already predicted that dephasing can assist — rather than destroy — transport; the chiral edge modes observed here may be the physical realization of that prediction under realistic vibrational dressing[12].

Beyond photosynthesis, the symmetry-protection argument generalizes to any ordered chromophore lattice, suggesting that topology — long regarded as a low-temperature condensed-matter curiosity — may be a general engineering principle of efficient biological energy transduction[7].

Methods & Data

All experiments were performed on recombinantly expressed FMO trimers from Chlorobaculum tepidum. Transient absorption and 2DES measurements used the apparatus described in Methods detail; coupling parameters were extracted by fitting the measured beating spectra to the two-dimensional SSH Hamiltonian[9] with a Bloch-state classifier[3].
